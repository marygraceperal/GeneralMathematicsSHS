Learning Overview

Statistics helps us answer questions using data. A complete investigation is more than applying formulas: it includes collecting appropriate information, selecting meaningful summaries, interpreting results, and acknowledging limitations.

Learning targets: identify data types and measurement levels; compute mean, median, mode, range, variance, standard deviation, quartiles and IQR; construct grouped frequency tables; interpret results; and complete statistical investigations.

All example data are fictional and intended for classroom practice. Survey participation should be voluntary; avoid collecting names or sensitive personal information.

3. Ungrouped Data: Eight Measures

Ungrouped data retain each individual observation. We will use one consistent set of seven quiz scores:

2, 4, 4, 6, 8, 10, 15

These values are sorted from smallest to largest; n = 7 and their sum is 49.

1. Mean

x̄ = Σx / n

Calculation: (2 + 4 + 4 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 15) / 7 = 49 / 7 = 7

Interpretation: The arithmetic average is 7 points.

2. Median

Odd n: position = (n + 1) / 2

Calculation: (7 + 1) / 2 = 4th observation = 6

Interpretation: The middle score is 6. For even n, average the two middle values.

3. Mode

Value with the highest frequency

Calculation: 4 occurs twice; all other values once → 4

Interpretation: Four is the most common score.

4. Range

R = maximum − minimum

Calculation: 15 − 2 = 13

Interpretation: The scores span 13 points.

5. Sample variance

s² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)

Calculation: Squared deviations total 118; s² = 118 / 6 = 19.67

Interpretation: Variation is 19.67 squared points.

6. Sample standard deviation

s = √s²

Calculation: √(118 / 6) = 4.43 points

Interpretation: The sample spread is expressed in the original score units.

7. Quartiles

Q1 = median of lower half; Q2 = median; Q3 = median of upper half

Calculation: Lower: 2, 4, 4 → Q1 = 4; center Q2 = 6; upper: 8, 10, 15 → Q3 = 10

Interpretation: The ordered values are divided into approximately four parts.

Quartile illustration: [2, 4, 4] | 6 | [8, 10, 15]. We use the median-of-halves convention excluding the overall median when n is odd. Software may use a different quartile convention.

8. IQR

IQR = Q3 − Q1

Calculation: 10 − 4 = 6

Interpretation: The middle 50% spans 6 points.

4. Constructing Class Intervals: Six Steps

Problem: Organize the following 30 quiz scores into a grouped frequency distribution:

12, 18, 25, 30, 22, 15, 28, 35, 40, 19, 24, 31, 27, 33, 20, 16, 38, 42, 29, 26, 14, 21, 34, 37, 23, 17, 32, 36, 39, 45

Step 1 — Determine the range.

Highest = 45; lowest = 12. R = H − L = 45 − 12 = 33

Step 2 — Estimate the number of classes using Sturges’ Rule.
k = 1 + 3.322 log₁₀(n) = 1 + 3.322 log₁₀(30) ≈ 5.91 → 6 classes
Step 3 — Determine class width.

For integer-valued scores, include both endpoints:

w = ceiling[(H − L + 1) / k] = ceiling(34 / 6) = 6
Step 4 — Construct nonoverlapping intervals.

Start at 12, include six integer scores per class, and continue until 45 is covered.

Step 5 — Tally and count the observations.

Each score must appear in exactly one class. Add cumulative frequency as a running total; verify Σf = 30.

Step 6 — Calculate class boundaries and midpoints.

For integer-valued scores, subtract 0.5 from the lower class limit and add 0.5 to the upper limit. Midpoint = (lower limit + upper limit) / 2.

For measurements recorded to the nearest tenth, class-boundary adjustments are typically 0.05, not 0.5. Sturges’ Rule and class widths are useful guidelines rather than rigid requirements.

1. Mean

x̄ ≈ Σfx / N

Substitution: 455 / 20 = 22.75

Interpretation: Estimated average score.

2. Median

Md ≈ L + [(N/2 − CF) / fₘ]h

Substitution: N/2 = 10, median class 20–24. L = 19.5, CF = 6, fₘ = 6, h = 5. Therefore 19.5 + [(10 − 6)/6] × 5 = 22.83

Interpretation: Estimated middle value.

3. Mode

Mo ≈ L + [(f₁ − f₀)/(2f₁ − f₀ − f₂)]h

Substitution: Modal class 20–24. L = 19.5; f₁ = 6, f₀ = 4, f₂ = 5. 19.5 + [(6 − 4)/(12 − 4 − 5)] × 5 = 22.83

Interpretation: Estimated most frequent value within the modal class.

4. Range

R ≈ highest class boundary − lowest class boundary

Substitution: 34.5 − 9.5 = 25

Interpretation: Boundary-based estimate; exact range is unknown.

5. Sample variance

s² ≈ [Σfx² − (Σfx)²/N] / (N − 1)

Substitution: [11,065 − (455²/20)] / 19 = 713.75 / 19 = 37.57

Interpretation: Estimated squared spread.

6. Sample standard deviation

s ≈ √s²

Substitution: √37.5658 = 6.13 points

Interpretation: Estimated spread in score units.

7. Quartiles

Qᵣ ≈ L + [(rN/4 − CF)/fᵩ]h, r = 1, 2, 3

Substitution: Q1: 14.5 + [(5 − 2)/4] × 5 = 18.25. Q2 = 22.83. Q3: 24.5 + [(15 − 12)/5] × 5 = 27.50.

Interpretation: Approximate 25th, 50th and 75th percentile positions.

8. IQR

IQR ≈ Q3 − Q1

Substitution: 27.50 − 18.25 = 9.25

Interpretation: Estimated spread of the middle half.

Grouped range calculated using extreme class boundaries is an approximation, not the exact maximum-minus-minimum. For an entire population, variance uses N rather than N − 1. Grouped quartiles depend on the interpolation convention.

6. Using Technology to Calculate Statistics

For ungrouped numerical observations in spreadsheet cells A2:A11, the following Excel or compatible spreadsheet functions can help check calculations:

MeasureSpreadsheet formula
Mean=AVERAGE(A2:A11)
Median=MEDIAN(A2:A11)
Mode=MODE.SNGL(A2:A11)
Range=MAX(A2:A11)-MIN(A2:A11)
Sample variance=VAR.S(A2:A11)
Sample standard deviation=STDEV.S(A2:A11)
Quartiles (inclusive convention)=QUARTILE.INC(A2:A11,1) and =QUARTILE.INC(A2:A11,3)
IQR (inclusive convention)=QUARTILE.INC(A2:A11,3)-QUARTILE.INC(A2:A11,1)
Spreadsheet quartiles may differ from the median-of-halves convention used earlier. State which method you use. To calculate grouped statistics, first construct midpoint and frequency columns; do not apply ungrouped formulas directly to the class limits.

7. Five Complete Statistical Investigations

Each example models the complete sequence: question → collection → organization → analysis → interpretation → conclusion.

1. Daily Transportation Expenses

Simple random sample · numerical ungrouped

Step 1 — Ask: Question: What do ten students spend on transportation in one day?

Step 2 — Collect: Record the observations through the stated method; participation is voluntary where relevant.

Step 3 — Organize: 10, 15, 15, 20, 20, 20, 25, 25, 30, 40

Step 4 — Analyze: Mean ₱22; median ₱20; mode ₱20; range ₱30; Q1 ₱15; Q3 ₱25; IQR ₱10.

Step 5 — Interpret: Typical expense is around ₱20–₱22 for this sample; the ₱40 expense raises the mean.

Step 6 — Conclude: State what the observations support, the relevant population, and one limitation of the investigation.

2. Preferred Bread Product

Convenience survey · nominal categorical

Step 1 — Ask: Question: Which bread is most preferred among 30 surveyed customers?

Step 2 — Collect: Record the observations through the stated method; participation is voluntary where relevant.

Step 3 — Organize: Pandesal 12; Ensaymada 8; Spanish bread 6; Cheese bread 4

Step 4 — Analyze: Percentages: 40%, 26.7%, 20%, 13.3%. Mode = Pandesal.

Step 5 — Interpret: Pandesal is most popular among respondents, but 60% chose another bread. No meaningful numerical mean.

Step 6 — Conclude: State what the observations support, the relevant population, and one limitation of the investigation.

Paired assessment · ungrouped numerical

9. Review, Interpretation and Key Reminders

Quick check

  1. Why is the median sometimes more representative than the mean?
  2. Why is a numerical mean inappropriate for bread preference categories?
  3. How does standard deviation differ from variance?
  4. Why are grouped mean and quartiles usually estimates?
  5. Why must the sum of class frequencies equal the number of observations?
  6. What does a large IQR tell you about the middle 50% of the data?
Show suggested responses
  1. The median is less affected by extreme values.
  2. Categories are labels, not numerical quantities.
  3. SD is the square root of variance and has original units; variance has squared units.
  4. Individual values are replaced by midpoints or interpolation assumptions.
  5. Each observation must be counted exactly once.
  6. The middle half of observations is more widely spread.
