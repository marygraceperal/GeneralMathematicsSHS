What are statistical measurements?

Statistical measurements are numerical values or descriptive measures used to summarize, organize, analyze, interpret, and compare data. They help us understand what is typical, how observations differ, where values are positioned, and what conclusions can reasonably be drawn from collected information.

Essential idea: Different research questions require different statistical measures. No single measure describes every feature of a data set.

1. Measures of Central Tendency

These measurements describe the center or typical value of a data set.

MeasureDefinitionPurpose and importance
MeanSum of all numerical observations divided by the number of observations.Finds the arithmetic average; useful for comparing average scores, expenses, income, and performance.
MedianMiddle value of an ordered data set, or the average of the two middle values.Identifies the central position; particularly useful when extreme values affect the mean.
ModeValue or category occurring most frequently.Identifies the most common observation, including categorical preferences.
Worked example: Daily transportation expenses

₱10, ₱15, ₱15, ₱20, ₱40

Mean: (10 + 15 + 15 + 20 + 40) ÷ 5 = ₱20
Median: middle observation = ₱15
Mode: most frequent observation = ₱15

Interpretation: The ₱40 expense raises the mean. The median may better represent a typical student's transportation expense.

2. Measures of Variability or Dispersion

These measurements describe how spread out the observations are.

MeasureDefinitionPurpose and importance
RangeHighest observation minus lowest observation.Provides a quick measure of total spread.
VarianceMeasure of average squared deviations from the mean, using the appropriate population or sample formula.Quantifies variability and supports further statistical analysis.
Standard deviationSquare root of the variance.Describes spread around the mean in the original units.
Interquartile range (IQR)Third quartile minus first quartile.Describes spread of the middle 50% and is less affected by extreme values.
Worked example: Same mean, different variability
Group A

10, 12, 14, 16, 18

Mean = 14
Range = 8
Sample SD ≈ 3.16

Group B

6, 10, 14, 18, 22

Mean = 14
Range = 16
Sample SD ≈ 6.32

Interpretation: Both groups have the same mean, but Group A's scores are more consistent. The mean alone does not describe the full distribution.

3. Measures of Position

These measurements describe where observations lie within an ordered distribution.

MeasureDefinitionPurpose and importance
QuartilesValues dividing ordered data into four approximately equal parts.Describe lower, middle, and upper positions and support spread analysis.
DecilesValues dividing ordered data into ten approximately equal parts.Describe relative standing in tenths.
PercentilesValues dividing ordered data into 100 approximately equal parts.Describe relative standing, often used in assessments and growth charts.

4. Measures of Distribution Shape

MeasureDefinitionPurpose and importance
SkewnessDegree and direction of asymmetry in a distribution.Indicates whether observations are approximately symmetric or have a longer tail on one side.
KurtosisDescribes tail heaviness and extreme-value behavior relative to a reference distribution.Helps analyze unusually extreme observations.

Skewness can help explain why a mean and median differ substantially.

5. Measures of Relationship

MeasureDefinitionPurpose and importance
Correlation coefficientMeasures strength and direction of an association, such as a linear relationship measured by Pearson's r.Helps investigate whether two variables tend to change together.
CovarianceMeasures how two numerical variables vary together.Shows whether larger values of one variable tend to accompany larger or smaller values of another.
Example: Study time and quiz scores may show positive correlation. However, correlation does not establish that study time alone caused higher scores.

6. Why Statistical Measurements Matter

  1. Summarize large amounts of information into understandable values.
  2. Identify typical observations, such as average scores or common expenses.
  3. Measure consistency and variation across groups or processes.
  4. Compare data sets beyond their averages.
  5. Identify unusual observations that deserve investigation.
  6. Support evidence-based decisions in education, business, health, and research.
  7. Communicate findings clearly using numerical summaries and sound interpretations.

7. Choosing the Appropriate Statistical Measure

Research purposeRecommended measure
Find the arithmetic averageMean
Find the middle observationMedian
Identify the most common value or categoryMode
Determine overall spreadRange
Quantify squared variationVariance
Measure spread in original unitsStandard deviation
Determine relative positionQuartiles, deciles, percentiles
Measure spread of the middle halfIQR
Describe asymmetrySkewness
Examine relationships between two variablesCorrelation
Key learning point: Choose the measure according to the data type, research question, presence of outliers, and purpose of the analysis. The eight core measures—mean, median, mode, range, variance, standard deviation, quartiles, and IQR—form a strong foundation for describing grouped and ungrouped data.
